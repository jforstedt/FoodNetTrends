# Historical county coverage research — 12 September 2026

Public primary sources resolve Fairfield's bacterial entry year. They also confirm
that early parasite surveillance cannot use the bacterial county footprint. They
do **not** yet establish a complete California parasite county/date ledger through
2003. This evidence review changes no eligibility rules, cases, denominators,
model specifications, fitted results, or dashboards.

## Connecticut bacterial coverage: resolved

The Connecticut Department of Public Health explicitly describes the sequence:
Hartford and New Haven initially, Fairfield added in 1997, statewide surveillance
from 1998. Its 2001 summary repeats that sequence in the footnote defining the
reported illnesses. This directly supports the supplied inventory's Fairfield
1997 entry. [Connecticut Epidemiologist, August 2001, page 13](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/departments-and-agencies/dph/dph/infectious_diseases/ctepinews/vol21no4pdf.pdf)

CDC's contemporary report for 1996 names Hartford/New Haven as the initial CT
counties and dates the addition of one Connecticut county to **January 1, 1997**.
Combined with DPH's named county, this establishes Fairfield's start date; the
identification of that unnamed county as Fairfield is a cross-source inference.
[CDC MMWR, Foodborne Diseases Active Surveillance Network, 1996, editorial note and catchment footnote](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00046981.htm)

An independent contemporary DPH report describes active laboratory surveillance
for Campylobacter among Hartford, New Haven, and Fairfield residents during 1997,
including county-specific rates. [Connecticut Epidemiologist, volume 18 number 2, pages 6–7](https://portal.ct.gov/dph/-/media/departments-and-agencies/dph/dph/infectious_diseases/ctepinews/vol18no2pdf.pdf)

| Period | Supported bacterial geographic footprint |
|---|---|
| 1996 | Hartford and New Haven |
| From January 1, 1997 | Hartford, New Haven, Fairfield |
| From 1998 | All eight historical Connecticut counties |

This does not assert surveillance for bacterial categories introduced later, such
as STEC non-O157, in every early year. Pathogen start dates remain a separate
condition from geographic coverage.

The retrospective [2009 FoodNet report, Table 1](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152214/cdc_152214_DS1.pdf)
compresses CT into original counties and rest-of-state in 1998, omitting the
intermediate Fairfield expansion. The [1997 surveillance MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00054940.htm)
describes three CT bacterial counties even when referring to 1996. Neither
conflicting summary should overwrite the explicit contemporary sequence. This is
an evidence-based reconciliation, not a claim that CDC issued a correction.

## Connecticut parasites: separate from bacterial geography

The 1997 MMWR specifically describes statewide CT surveillance for Cryptosporidium
and Cyclospora in 1997. The annual report's population table independently gives
CT a statewide parasite denominator (3,269,858), versus the smaller bacterial
catchment (2,450,656). Therefore, Fairfield's bacterial entry should not be used
to defer other CT counties' parasite coverage to 1998.
[CDC 1997 surveillance MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00054940.htm),
[1997 final report, PDF page 25](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152204/cdc_152204_DS1.pdf#page=25)

## California parasites: broader geography verified, full ledger unresolved

### What the annual reports establish

The 1997 final report's catchment table explicitly limits California parasite
surveillance before June 1, 1997 to Alameda, Contra Costa, and San Francisco
(population 3,002,632). The table gives 6,340,996 for the broader parasite
catchment. The contemporary MMWR describes eight California counties for the two
parasites, including the two bacterial counties. Neither passage names all five
additional counties or documents how a county model should handle their partial
year. [1997 final report, PDF page 25](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152204/cdc_152204_DS1.pdf#page=25),
[1997 surveillance MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00054940.htm)

Later annual reports independently retain substantially larger parasite than
bacterial populations:

| Reporting year | CA bacterial population | CA parasite population | Primary source |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1998 | 2,146,096 | 6,453,765 | [1998 final report, PDF page 33](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152192/cdc_152192_DS1.pdf#page=33) |
| 1999 | 2,162,359 | 6,507,482 | [1999 final report, PDF page 33](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152193/cdc_152193_DS1.pdf#page=33) |

These are historical published estimates, not a proposed population vintage for
new analyses. They establish distinct surveillance areas in those years; they do
not independently identify each county or prove unchanged coverage between them.

### Why the five names are plausible but not yet an approved ledger

The original FoodNet population-survey paper names Alameda, Contra Costa, Marin,
San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Solano, and Sonoma as its California
sampling area for July 1998–June 1999. Its methods are evidence about a **telephone
population survey**, so this county list cannot alone prove laboratory parasite
surveillance dates. It corroborates the geography of the five exception counties
in the supplied audit, but does not justify filling missing denominators or
creating zero-case county/year rows. [Imhoff et al., 2004, methods, page S220](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/38/Supplement_3/S219/355064)

The 2000–2003 annual narrative reports were also inspected. They describe selected
California counties but do not provide an explicit start/end schedule for each of
those five additional parasite counties. The 2003 report's Table 4 gives a single
California population series, including 3,213,848 for 2003; that table does not
explain the earlier broader parasite catchment or when its extra counties ceased
contributing. A case appearing in the raw extract through 2003 is insufficient
to settle this question. [2000 report](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152195/cdc_152195_DS1.pdf),
[2001 report](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152197/cdc_152197_DS1.pdf),
[2002 report](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152198/cdc_152198_DS1.pdf),
[2003 report, Table 4](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152199/cdc_152199_DS1.pdf)

No county-specific cessation dates or retrospective inclusion convention were
verified in this search. These exceptions remain historical coverage/denominator
questions, not evidence of invalid FIPS.

## Additional partial-year issue: Oregon parasites in 1997

Visual inspection of the scanned narrative found a footnote in Table 1B:
Oregon began parasite surveillance on July 1, 1997. This text is in a scanned page
and is absent from the downloaded PDF's searchable text layer. The relevant
location is **printed page 8, PDF page 7** (both one-based).
[1997 final report](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/152204/cdc_152204_DS1.pdf#page=7)

A full annual population or a July-start person-time fraction cannot be adopted
interchangeably: case inclusion dates and the estimand must agree. The research
does not impose a fraction or discard early data. A parser-only review would
have missed this condition.

## What remains before a real county fit

1. Record the now-supported Connecticut bacterial sequence separately from the
   1997 statewide parasite footprint.
2. Resolve California's five additional parasite counties' individual start/end
   dates and the treatment of their cases through 2003. Confirm whether the raw
   extract includes retrospective or out-of-catchment records.
3. Specify case-date filtering and exposure handling for California's June 1997
   expansion and Oregon's July 1997 parasite start. Preserve uncertainty until a
   defensible, documented analysis convention is selected.
4. Select population vintages and geography explicitly. The validated CT town
   aggregation resolves a population construction issue, not surveillance scope.

The supplied archive does not contain the needed case-date audit, and no public
county/date protocol resolving item 2 was located. The user does not need to run
another model to resolve these questions. Synthetic INLA installation, adjacency,
and model-interface checks can proceed independently.

For a later real-data pilot, the publicly documented stable **2004–2022** catchment
is a possible way to avoid the early transition years, subject to an explicit
analysis scope and complete matching county inputs. This is a proposed pilot
scope, not an implemented exclusion. CDC also documents the 2023 Colorado
expansion and a Yersinia exception, reinforcing that site geography and pathogen
coverage must be tracked separately. [CDC 2023 surveillance report, Table 1 footnotes](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/wr/pdfs/mm7326a1-H.pdf)

Research examined the cited CDC/DPH reports and original survey publication on
12 September 2026. Search-result publication timestamps were not substituted for
the publication dates printed in the reports. No communication to CDC, DPH, or
other people was sent.
